1Executive summary

Two concepts, carried at equal weight, and no recommendation between them. Exhibit 1 is four attached townhomes of about 1,201 sq ft, each with its own private driveway and attached garage. Exhibit 2 is one building of five one-bedroom apartments of about 680 sq ft over a shared parking court. Both are planned developments under Title 11 Chapter 18 at the 0.39 floor area ratio ceiling. Five dwelling units is the legal maximum on this parcel under any built form. One single-family home is achievable by right, with no hearing, under 11-11-4B2. The 10-unit target cannot be delivered.

Built form does not raise the unit ceiling. This is the first thing to be clear about. A developer will assume that connecting units, sharing walls or stacking flats buys more of them. It does not. The binding standard is lot area per dwelling unit (11-7H-1A), a land requirement indifferent to how units are arranged. The townhome scheme already shares party walls with no gaps at all, since four units at 19.04 ft is 76.16 ft exactly. The only route past five units is more land. Section 5.1 sets this out in full.

The parcel is 12,320 sq ft, 96.16 ft by 128.1 ft. The record carried its zoning as "OT", which is not a base district but the Old Town Preservation overlay. The Village's 2026 Old Town Zoning Map shows this block as R-2 OT SP 79-53, so the base district is R-2 and, per 11-11-4A, the R-2 bulk and height standards govern.

Floor area is the binding constraint, and Chapter 18 caps the relief. The R-2 yards leave a buildable envelope of about 3,663 sq ft while the R-2 FAR of 0.30 caps floor area at 3,696 sq ft, so a single storey filling the envelope already spends the whole allowance. A planned development can raise that, but 11-18-3C1 says in no event more than 30 percent over the district figure, fixing the maximum at 0.39, or 4,805 sq ft. 11-18-3B caps coverage at 25 percent, 3,080 sq ft. 11-18-3C2 cuts the minimum area per unit by 10 percent, and 10 percent again where parking is in the building proper, taking the R-4 two-bedroom standard of 2,800 sq ft per unit to 2,268 sq ft. 12,320 / 2,268 = 5.43, the five-unit ceiling.

Where the two concepts genuinely differ. Four townhomes fit the width comfortably at 19.04 ft per unit and support two-bedroom homes; five townhomes would be 15.23 ft wide, which is below a workable width. The apartment form is not width constrained and therefore reaches five units. It pays for them in unit size: after five mandatory indoor parking spaces and circulation, the 4,805 sq ft allowance leaves about 680 sq ft per unit. That is a hard finding, not a caution: five two-bedroom apartments cannot be delivered. Five at the 850 sq ft two-bedroom minimum plus garage and circulation needs about 5,650 sq ft gross, FAR 0.459, or 0.418 even crediting the full 500 sq ft garage exclusion. Both exceed the 0.39 ceiling. Five one-bedroom units at about 680 sq ft clear the 650 sq ft minimum and do work.

The two concepts also carry different regulatory risk, and this is the sharpest difference between them. Under 11-18-3C the density standard follows the district the Village Board deems most similar in nature and function. The whole calculation assumes that district is R-4; if the Board names R-3 instead, at 8,000 sq ft per attached unit, the yield collapses to one unit. That is OI-14, the largest open risk in this report. 11-7H-2 defines the R-4 permitted use as a multiple-family dwelling of five or more units, so Exhibit 2 is literally an R-4 permitted use on its face, while a townhome row invites the R-3 "group, row and town houses" characterisation instead.

The 10-unit target did not come from the site; it is the planning tool's schema minimum, entered as a probe. At the most permissive density the code allows, ten units need 22,680 sq ft against 12,320 available: the site is 1.84 times too small even at maximum relief. Sixteen open items are carried in Section 10 and both concepts are subject to all of them. The only durations in this report are the two eighteen month periods at 11-18-4C1 and 11-18-6, and both are post-approval validity periods, not processing times.

Immediate next steps, common to both concepts

Put the "most similar district" question to the Village at the tentative approval stage under 11-18-4A, before design expense is incurred, alongside written zoning confirmation and a boundary survey. Those three items settle the largest uncertainties at the lowest cost, and the answer to the first will itself inform which concept to carry forward.

The record did not answer that question correctly, and a county GIS lookup cannot answer it at all. 2.1 What the record said

The parcel record carried the zoning as "OT", user confirmed. Read as a base district, "OT" produces nothing: Bloomingdale's zoning code contains no district coded "OT" standing alone. A search of the code by that letter pair returns no bulk table, no permitted use list and no yard requirements, which is exactly the pattern you see when a code has been recorded as a base district but is in fact something else.

2.2 What "OT" actually is

Title 11 Chapter 11 of the Village Code is "OTP Old Town Preservation District". OT is that chapter's map notation. The Village's zoning map makes this explicit in its labelling convention, where each parcel is written as a base district followed by suffixes.

2.4 Why a DuPage County GIS zoning lookup returns nothing

DuPage County's ArcGIS zoning service is Zoning/UnincorporatedZoningData. As the name states, it covers unincorporated DuPage County only. 118 S First Street sits inside the incorporated Village of Bloomingdale, so the county layer simply does not carry zoning for it. A query against that service returns an empty result, and an empty result is easy to misread as "no zoning on record" when it actually means "not this map's jurisdiction". Within a municipality the Village's own adopted zoning map is the authority, and that is the source relied on above.

The parcel record on the source project has since been corrected from OT to R-2, with the original recorded value preserved.

2.5 The hinge: which bulk standards apply

Having established that the base district is R-2 and the overlay is OTP, the next question is which set of bulk and height numbers governs. Section 11-11-4A answers it directly.

With the exception of uses described in subsection B of this section, all uses in the old town preservation district shall be special uses, and shall be allowed in the manner for special uses and in keeping with the terms and conditions of this title. Unless otherwise specified under the terms of a special use permit, the building height and bulk regulations of the underlying zoning classification shall remain in effect. Said uses shall be consistent with the land use recommendations contained in the old town plan attached as exhibit A to ordinance 2006-03. Bloomingdale Village Code, 11-11-4A

Two consequences follow, and both matter to the yield:

  1. OTP is an overlay, so bulk and height come from the underlying district. Here that is R-2, and the governing bulk table is 11-7D-1. This is why Section 4 of this report is an R-2 table and not an Old Town table: Chapter 11 does not publish its own bulk numbers.
  2. A special use permit can lawfully set different bulk. The clause "unless otherwise specified under the terms of a special use permit" is written into the ordinance. It is the route to anything above the R-2 baseline, and it is a mechanism the code provides rather than an exception the applicant invents. This is the legal foundation for the floor area relief sought in Scenarios C and F, subject to the Chapter 18 ceiling in Section 4.6.

4.6 Planned developments, Title 11 Chapter 18: the relief, and where it stops

Planned developments are a permitted special use in the Old Town Preservation District under 11-11-4D5, and Chapter 18 governs them. The chapter matters twice over: it makes any yield above three units possible, and it puts an absolute ceiling on it.

11-18-3 provides that the Planning and Zoning Commission may recommend, and the Village Board may authorize, exceptions to the applicable regulations of Title 11 within the boundaries of such a development, on the evidence set out in the subsections that follow. 11-18-3A requires the exception to be solely for the purpose of encouraging a desirable living environment no less beneficial to the residents or occupants of the development, and of neighbouring properties, than would be obtained under the bulk regulations of Title 11 for buildings developed on separate zoning lots.

That maximum parcel coverage in a residential planned development shall not exceed twenty five percent (25%) in the residential zoning districts. Bloomingdale Village Code, 11-18-3B
In no event shall the floor area ratio exceed more than thirty percent (30%) over that prescribed in this title for the district in which it is located. Bloomingdale Village Code, 11-18-3C1
In no event shall the minimum square footage per dwelling unit requirements applicable to such districts be decreased by more than ten percent (10%). A further decrease of not more than ten percent (10%) on the already reduced square footage per dwelling unit may be permitted in the event that parking facilities are provided either underground or in the building proper, i.e., for each underground or on building parking space, an equivalent reduction may be permitted to the allowable limit of ten percent (10%). Bloomingdale Village Code, 11-18-3C2

11-18-3C further provides that other standards for density, yard regulations, parking, loading and screening for a planned development "shall be governed by the standards of the residential, business or manufacturing zoning district(s) most similar in nature and function to the proposed planned development as determined by the village board." That sentence is the hinge of the whole yield calculation, and Section 5 returns to it.

Two things a reader will assume, and should not

There is no minimum site area for a planned development in Chapter 18. A 0.28 acre planned development is therefore not excluded by size, and the usual assumption that a planned development needs acreage does not hold here.

The relief is capped, not negotiable. 11-18-3C1 and 11-18-3C2 both use the words "in no event". A floor area ratio of 0.40, the figure R-3 and R-4 apply in their own districts, is not available through a planned development on an R-2 base, because 0.39 is the arithmetic limit. Any concept needing more than 0.39 has no route through this chapter.

5.1 Built form does not change the unit ceiling

Connecting units, sharing walls or stacking flats does not produce more of them. This is the single most likely misconception to bring to this page, so it is disposed of before the scenarios begin.

  • The binding standard is a land standard. 11-7H-1A expresses R-4 density as lot area per dwelling unit. It is a requirement about ground, not about geometry, and it is indifferent to whether units sit side by side, stack vertically, or share party walls.
  • The townhome scheme already shares party walls, with no gaps. Four units at 19.04 ft across a 76.16 ft buildable width is 76.16 ft exactly. There are no side yards between attached units; the 20 ft combined side yard requirement applies only at the two ends of the row, against the neighbouring properties north and south.
  • So there is no space between units to remove, and removing it would gain nothing.
  • Five units is the ceiling under every built form. The only route past it is more land.

What built form does change is whether the ceiling can actually be reached, and at what unit size. That is the real subject of Scenarios D and F, and of the comparison in Section 7.

8.4 The planned development procedure in detail, 11-18-4

Chapter 18 runs a two stage process, and the first stage is easy to miss. It is also the cheapest place to resolve the largest risk in this report.

Stage one: tentative approval, 11-18-4A

The developer submits a tentative planned development to the Planning and Zoning Commission for approval in principle, which the Commission must approve before an application for a planned development special use permit is submitted. Written consent of all property owners within the development must be on file with the Village before study of a tentative plan is even commenced. Approval in principle covers the general acceptability of the land uses proposed and their interrelationships; it does not endorse the precise location of uses, the configuration of parcels, or engineering feasibility. The stated purpose of the stage is to let the developer obtain the Commission's advice and assistance before incurring the expense of a plan.

Tentative plan contents: proposed land uses, population densities and building intensities; the proposed circulation pattern indicating public and private streets and off street parking ratios; proposed parks, playgrounds, school sites and other open spaces; a market analysis of proposed commercial uses if the property is not zoned commercial at submittal; delineation of the units to be constructed in progression, if any; and the relation to future land use in the surrounding area and the Village comprehensive plan.

Stage two: the planned development special use application, 11-18-4B

Requires at minimum sixteen items: a topographic and boundary line map of the project locating its relationship to surrounding properties; the pattern of public and private roads, driveways, parking facilities and intended design standards; size, area and location of lots or proposed building groups; location, type and size of landscaping; use, type, size and location of structures; location of sewer and water facilities; architectural drawings and sketches illustrating design and character; location of recreational and open space area; existing and proposed storm drainage pattern; statistical data pertinent to a comprehensive evaluation; organizational details of any property owners association; aspects of the maintenance of common open space areas; a schedule for development of units to be constructed in progression; a list of use and bulk exceptions requested; engineering feasibility studies as necessary; and relation to future land use in the surrounding area and the Village comprehensive plan.

Public hearing, 11-18-4C1. The Planning and Zoning Commission shall hold a public hearing on the application. It may approve, disapprove, or recommend changes or revisions it deems desirable, and forwards its report and recommendation to the Village Board.

Written agreement of restrictions, 11-18-4C2. A written agreement of restrictions signed by the owners must be submitted with the final plan, assuring the Village that the development will be carried out in full compliance with the final plan and within the submitted construction time schedule. Once the plan is authorised by the Village Board, no modification may be made without Village Board authorisation.

Permits, 11-18-5. Whenever the Village Board approves the final plan, the Building and Zoning Official issues the necessary permits for all of the project, or for such units as are to be constructed.

Performance security, 11-18-7. At the time the permit is granted, the Village Board makes arrangements with the applicant to ensure the public improvements and grants of easement shown on the approved final plan are accomplished at the scheduled times.
